Why multi-agent evaluation is harder

With one agent, you can score its runs. With many agents, the thing you care about often lives between them.

Emergence

System behaviour arises from interactions and is not written in any single agent.

Self-interest

Each agent maximises its own utility; the group outcome can still be poor.

Distribution

No agent sees the whole system, so there is no single place to measure.

Non-determinism

Message timing, concurrency and, for LLMs, sampling change results from run to run.

Scale

A protocol that works for 5 agents may collapse at 500.

Openness

Agents may join, leave or fail during a run.

Source: Wooldridge (2009), Ch. 1 and Ch. 11; Shoham & Leyton-Brown (2008), Multiagent Systems, Ch. 1.

Criteria for judging group outcomes

Let ω be an outcome and ui(ω) the utility agent i gets from it. Five criteria answer five different questions, and a good report states which ones it uses.

CriterionQuestion it answersHow to compute
Utilitarian social welfareIs the total benefit as large as possible?sw(ω) = Σi ui(ω)
Egalitarian social welfareHow well off is the worst-off agent?mini ui(ω)
Pareto efficiencyCan anyone be made better off without making someone worse off?ω is efficient if no ω′ is at least as good for all and better for one
Stability (Nash equilibrium)Does any agent want to deviate on its own?No agent gains by changing only its own strategy
Individual rationalityIs every agent better off participating than staying out?ui(ω) ≥ utility of not participating

Source: Wooldridge (2009), Ch. 11; Russell & Norvig (2020), Ch. 18; Shoham & Leyton-Brown (2008), Ch. 3.

Worked example: when stable is not efficient

The Prisoner’s Dilemma with Wooldridge’s payoff values. Each cell shows (row agent i, column agent j).

j cooperatesj defects
i cooperates3, 3
sw = 6 · Pareto efficient		0, 5
sw = 5
i defects5, 0
sw = 5		2, 2
sw = 4 · Nash equilibrium
  • Whatever j does, i earns more by defecting (5 > 3 and 2 > 0). The same holds for j. So (Defect, Defect) is the only Nash equilibrium: it is stable.
  • It is not Pareto efficient. (Cooperate, Cooperate) makes both agents better off.
  • Social welfare is 4 at the equilibrium against 6 at the best outcome.

Lesson for evaluation: a protocol can be stable and still waste value. Report both stability and welfare.

Price of anarchy: the cost of selfishness

PoA = cost of the worst equilibrium ÷ cost of the optimal outcome

PoA = 1 means selfish agents reach the best outcome on their own. PoA > 1 means decentralisation has a price. Use it to decide whether a central coordinator is worth its overhead.

Pigou’s routing network

One unit of traffic travels from s to t. Road 1 is wide and always costs 1. Road 2 is narrow and costs x, where x is the share of traffic on it.

  • Selfish equilibrium. Road 2 never costs more than 1, so every driver prefers it. Everyone takes Road 2 and the average cost is 1.
  • Optimum. With a share x on Road 2 the average cost is (1 − x)·1 + x·x. It is smallest at x = ½, giving ½ + ¼ = ¾.
  • Price of anarchy = 1 ÷ ¾ = 4/3 ≈ 1.33. Selfish routing costs a third more than coordinated routing.

Roughgarden and Tardos showed that for any network whose road costs are linear like these, selfish routing is never worse than 4/3 of the optimum. Steeper, non-linear costs push the price higher; the interactive version lets you see that.

Evaluating a protocol or mechanism

Use these seven properties as a checklist when you compare auctions, voting rules or negotiation protocols in a report.

PropertyQuestion
Guaranteed successDoes the protocol always reach an agreement?
Maximises social welfareIs the agreed outcome the best for the group?
Pareto efficiencyIs no value left on the table?
Individual rationalityDo agents gain by following the protocol?
StabilityDo agents have an incentive to follow it honestly?
SimplicityAre computation and communication low for each agent?
DistributionCan it run without a single point of failure?

Source: Wooldridge (2009), Part III “Multiagent Decision Making” (protocol properties, after Rosenschein & Zlotkin, 1994 and Sandholm, 1999).

Read the chart carefully. The lecture plots n = 5, 10, 20, 40, 80. Each step doubles n, so the straight line 3n + 1 looks like it curves upward. Message growth is linear, not exponential. The interactive version lets you switch between the two axis styles.

A central allocator needs about 2 messages per task, but it is a single point of failure and it needs global information. That is exactly the trade-off the protocol checklist asks you to report.

Also measure: allocation cost compared with the optimal allocation, time to award, and success when a contractor fails after winning.

Self-check quiz

Choose an answer for each question, then check. Every answer comes with a short explanation.

1. In the Prisoner’s Dilemma, (Defect, Defect) is…

No agent gains by deviating alone, so it is stable. (Cooperate, Cooperate) is better for both, so it is not Pareto efficient.

2. Which criterion asks how well off the worst-off agent is?

Egalitarian welfare is the minimum utility across agents, a fairness measure.

3. In Pigou’s network the selfish cost is 1 and the optimal cost is ¾. The price of anarchy is…

PoA = 1 ÷ ¾ = 4/3 ≈ 1.33.

4. How many messages does one Contract Net task need with 20 contractors?

3n + 1 = 3 × 20 + 1 = 61.

5. A protocol keeps working when any one agent crashes. Which checklist property does that show?

Distribution means no single point of failure.
