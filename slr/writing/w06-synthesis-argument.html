SLR WRITING MODULES · W6

From Summary to Synthesis

Building an argument and a clear contribution from many studies.

Learning outcomes

  • Turn study-by-study summaries into synthesised paragraphs
  • Write a specific contribution statement
  • Use a conceptual framework to express the review’s contribution

Key points

Summary versus synthesis

  • A summary reports what each study found. A synthesis compares studies, explains patterns and contradictions, and states what the body of evidence shows.
  • Each Results paragraph should start with a synthesised claim, then support it with several studies, then qualify it.

Paragraph pattern

  • Claim: the pattern across studies.
  • Evidence: which studies support it, with counts.
  • Contrast: studies that disagree, and possible reasons (design, context, quality).
  • Implication: what this means for the research question.

Contribution

  • State in one or two sentences what the review adds beyond existing reviews: new scope, newer evidence, appraisal, a framework or an agenda.
  • Revisit the contribution in the Discussion and Conclusion.

Conceptual framework

  • A figure linking themes, conditions and outcomes is often the most cited part of an SLR.
  • Mark relationships that no study has tested; they are your research gaps.

Example: Synthesised paragraph

WeakStudy A found the tool useful. Study B found no difference. Study C interviewed students.
StrongerEvidence on learning gains was mixed: two controlled pilots reported higher test scores [S01, S04], whereas a larger observational study found no difference [S02]. All were single-site and none measured retention, so the effect on learning remains uncertain.
