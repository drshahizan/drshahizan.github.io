Key guidance

ApproachUse whenWhat it involves
Narrative synthesisHeterogeneous designs, contexts and outcomesStructured description of patterns, consistencies and differences supported by tables (Popay et al., 2006).
Thematic synthesisQualitative or mixed findings; RQs about experiences, factors, challengesLine-by-line coding → descriptive themes → analytical themes (Thomas & Harden, 2008).
Framework synthesisA recognised model or taxonomy already existsMap evidence onto an a-priori framework, then refine it (e.g. best-fit framework synthesis).
Synthesis without meta-analysis (SWiM)Quantitative outcomes that cannot be pooledGroup studies, standardise metrics and report direction of effect transparently (Campbell et al., 2020).
Meta-analysisComparable designs, populations and outcome measures with effect sizesStatistical pooling; requires heterogeneity assessment and is beyond most computing SLRs.
Bibliometric / mappingRQs about publication trends, venues, collaboration, topic clustersDescriptive counts and science-mapping (co-word, co-citation) to complement, not replace, synthesis.
  • Most SLRs in computing combine a descriptive mapping (who, when, where, how) with a narrative or thematic synthesis for the substantive RQs.
  • Decide the unit of grouping before you write: by RQ, by technique, by application domain, by outcome or by theme.
  • State how study quality is used in the synthesis — e.g. sensitivity reading, weighting of claims, or reporting findings separately for high-quality studies.
