SLR WRITING TOOLKIT · STAGE 3

Weak vs Stronger SLR Sections

Ten side-by-side examples, from title to conclusion, showing how to make each part of an SLR precise, transparent and evidence-based.

All examples are invented, based on a synthetic review of AI feedback in programming education. Use them as models for structure and precision, not as text to copy. For general research articles, see Polishing with AI · Weak vs stronger sections ↗.
TitleAbstract (methods sentence)Introduction (gap)Methods (search)Methods (selection)Methods (quality appraisal)Results (synthesis)DiscussionLimitationsConclusion

Title

WeakAI feedback in programming: a review
StrongerEffects of AI-generated feedback on novice programmers’ performance and help-seeking: a systematic literature review
  • Names the population, the intervention and the outcomes.
  • Identifies the article as a systematic review (PRISMA item 1).

Abstract (methods sentence)

WeakWe reviewed many recent articles from several databases about AI feedback.
StrongerWe searched Scopus, Web of Science and IEEE Xplore (January 2016 to June 2026) and included 42 empirical studies, appraised with the MMAT, and synthesised thematically.
  • Names the sources and the date range.
  • Gives the number of included studies, the appraisal tool and the synthesis method, as PRISMA 2020 for Abstracts requires.

Introduction (gap)

WeakThere is a lot of research on AI in education, but more research is needed.
StrongerThree earlier reviews examined AI tutors in general but none focused on feedback for novice programmers, and none appraised study quality. Since the latest review (2023), at least 20 new studies have appeared.
  • States what existing reviews covered and what they missed.
  • Justifies a new review with evidence rather than a general claim.

Methods (search)

WeakRelevant articles were searched in Google Scholar using keywords such as AI and programming.
StrongerWe searched Scopus, Web of Science Core Collection and IEEE Xplore on 15 June 2026 using three concept groups combined with AND (Supplementary Table S2), supplemented by backward and forward citation searching.
  • Names every source and the date last searched.
  • Points to the full, reproducible strings (PRISMA items 6 and 7).

Methods (selection)

WeakThe articles were screened and irrelevant ones were removed.
StrongerTwo reviewers independently screened 300 titles and abstracts (κ = 0.81) and 80 full texts in Rayyan; disagreements were resolved by discussion.
  • Reports how many reviewers, whether independent, and the tool used.
  • Gives agreement and how disagreements were resolved (PRISMA item 8).

Methods (quality appraisal)

WeakThe quality of the studies was checked.
StrongerTwo reviewers appraised each study with the MMAT (five criteria, scored yes/partly/no); studies scoring below 60% were retained but their findings were flagged in the synthesis.
  • Names the tool, the criteria and the scoring.
  • Says how quality results were used, not only that appraisal was done.

Results (synthesis)

WeakAhmad et al. (2023) found the tool useful. Brown and Lee (2024) used ChatGPT and students liked it. Chen (2022) found no difference in grades.
StrongerEvidence on learning gains was mixed: two controlled pilots reported higher immediate test scores [S01, S04], whereas a larger observational study found no difference in final marks [S02]. All three were single-site and none measured retention.
  • Starts with the synthesised finding, not with a study.
  • Compares studies, notes contradictions and the limits of the evidence.

Discussion

WeakThe results show that AI feedback is useful and students like it.
StrongerOur findings extend the review by Author (2023), which focused on general AI tutors, by showing that perceived usefulness is well supported but evidence of learning gains rests on short, single-site studies.
  • Interprets rather than repeats the results.
  • Compares explicitly with earlier reviews and qualifies the strength of evidence.

Limitations

WeakThis review has some limitations.
StrongerThis review was limited to English-language, peer-reviewed studies, so relevant grey literature may have been missed. Title and abstract screening of 20% of records by a single reviewer may also have introduced selection bias.
  • Names each limitation and its likely effect.
  • Separates limitations of the evidence from limitations of the review process (PRISMA item 23).

Conclusion

WeakIn conclusion, AI feedback proves to be very effective and should be used by all universities.
StrongerAI feedback is valued by novice programmers, but current evidence does not show lasting learning gains. Multi-site, longitudinal studies that measure retention are needed before institutions adopt it at scale.
  • Answers the research questions without overstating.
  • Ends with a specific, evidence-based implication.
