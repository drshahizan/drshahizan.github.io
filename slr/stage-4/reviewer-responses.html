SLR WRITING TOOLKIT · STAGE 4

Responding to Reviewers of an SLR

How to answer the comments that systematic reviews most often receive, with a response letter template and model replies.

The model replies use invented numbers and section references. Replace every detail with what you actually changed, and never promise a change you have not made.

Principles

  • Answer every comment, in the order the reviewer gave them.
  • Thank the reviewers once, at the start of the letter, not in every reply.
  • Quote the revised text and give its location (page and line numbers).
  • Disagree politely, with evidence or a methodological reference, and consider a small change that addresses the concern.
  • Never promise a change you have not made.
  • For an SLR, check that every change is reflected everywhere: abstract, methods, PRISMA diagram, tables and supplementary files.
  • Use AI only for tone and clarity, never to decide your position; check the journal’s AI policy.

Response letter template

Copy the template, then add each reviewer’s comments in order.

Common reviewer comments on SLRs, with model replies

Eleven comments that reviewers frequently raise about systematic reviews.

1. Reviewer comment

“The search strategy is not reproducible.”

Why reviewers ask: Search transparency is PRISMA item 7. Reviewers want to rerun your search.

Model reply
Thank you for this comment. We agree that the search was not reported in enough detail. We now report the full search string for each database, including field codes, limits and the date last searched (Supplementary Table S2), and summarise the strategy in Section 3.2.

Change made: Added full search strings and dates to Supplementary Table S2; revised Section 3.2 (p. 6, lines 118–140).

2. Reviewer comment

“Only one or two databases were searched; relevant studies may have been missed.”

Why reviewers ask: Coverage affects the completeness of the evidence base.

Model reply
We thank the reviewer for raising this point. To improve coverage, we additionally searched IEEE Xplore and the ACM Digital Library, and performed backward and forward citation searching of the included studies. This identified 6 additional eligible studies. The PRISMA diagram (Figure 1), Table 1 and the results have been updated accordingly.

Change made: Two databases and citation searching added; Figure 1, Table 1 and Sections 4.1–4.3 updated.

3. Reviewer comment

“The search is out of date.”

Why reviewers ask: Reviews often take months to publish; editors expect searches within about 12 months.

Model reply
We agree. We re-ran all searches on [date] using the same strategy. The update identified 74 new records, of which 5 met the eligibility criteria and have been included. All counts, figures and tables have been updated, and the new search date is reported in the abstract and Section 3.2.

Change made: Search updated; abstract, Section 3.2, Figure 1 and Tables 1–3 revised.

4. Reviewer comment

“Screening was done by a single reviewer, which increases the risk of bias.”

Why reviewers ask: PRISMA item 8 asks how many reviewers screened and whether independently.

Model reply
Thank you. A second reviewer has now independently screened a random sample of 20% of the title and abstract records and all full texts. Agreement was substantial (Cohen’s κ = 0.78), and disagreements were resolved by discussion. We report this in Section 3.3 and acknowledge the single-reviewer title and abstract screening as a limitation in Section 5.4.

Change made: Section 3.3 (p. 7, lines 150–158) and limitations (p. 18, lines 402–405) revised.

5. Reviewer comment

“Quality appraisal is missing, or low-quality studies were included without comment.”

Why reviewers ask: Without appraisal, readers cannot judge the strength of the evidence (PRISMA items 11 and 18).

Model reply
We thank the reviewer for this important point. We have now appraised all included studies using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT). Results are reported per study in the new Table 2. In the synthesis, we indicate where findings rely mainly on lower-quality studies, and we discuss this in Section 5.3.

Change made: New Table 2; Sections 3.5, 4.4 and 5.3 added or revised.

6. Reviewer comment

“Why was no meta-analysis performed?”

Why reviewers ask: Reviewers check that the synthesis method is justified, not just named.

Model reply
Thank you for this question. A meta-analysis was not appropriate because the included studies used different designs (experimental, survey and qualitative) and measured different outcomes with non-comparable instruments. Following the SWiM guideline, we used a structured narrative synthesis and grouped studies by outcome. We have added this justification to Section 3.6.

Change made: Justification added to Section 3.6 (p. 9, lines 201–209).

7. Reviewer comment

“The results describe studies one by one rather than synthesising them.”

Why reviewers ask: A list of summaries is the most common weakness of SLR results sections.

Model reply
We appreciate this comment. We have restructured Section 4 by theme rather than by study. Each sub-section now compares findings across studies, notes contradictory evidence and indicates the strength of evidence. A new synthesis table (Table 3) summarises the themes and supporting studies.

Change made: Section 4 restructured; new Table 3.

8. Reviewer comment

“The contribution is unclear compared with existing reviews.”

Why reviewers ask: Editors desk-reject reviews that duplicate recent ones.

Model reply
Thank you. We have added a paragraph to the Introduction that compares this review with the three most closely related reviews (Table A1 in the appendix), showing differences in scope, time frame and synthesis. The contribution is now stated explicitly at the end of the Introduction and revisited in Section 5.

Change made: Introduction (p. 3, lines 55–74), new Table A1, Section 5.1.

9. Reviewer comment

“The numbers in the PRISMA diagram do not match the text.”

Why reviewers ask: Inconsistent counts make reviewers question the whole review.

Model reply
Thank you for noticing this inconsistency, and we apologise for the error. The correct number of included studies is 42. We have corrected the abstract, Section 4.1 and Figure 1, and checked all other counts against our screening records.

Change made: Abstract, Section 4.1 and Figure 1 corrected.

10. Reviewer comment

“Restricting the review to English-language, peer-reviewed studies may introduce bias.”

Why reviewers ask: Language and publication bias are standard limitations.

Model reply
We agree that this restriction may have excluded relevant evidence. We retained it because [reason, e.g. the review team could not reliably appraise studies in other languages], and we now discuss the possible effect of language and publication bias in the limitations (Section 5.4).

Change made: Limitations expanded (p. 18, lines 406–412).

11. Reviewer comment

“Was AI used in the screening, extraction or writing of this review?”

Why reviewers ask: Journals increasingly ask how AI tools were used, not only for language.

Model reply
Thank you for this question. ASReview was used to prioritise records during title and abstract screening; all records were nevertheless screened by a human reviewer. [Tool] was used to improve the language of selected paragraphs. No AI tool was used to extract data, appraise quality or draw conclusions. This is now stated in Section 3.3 and in the AI disclosure statement.

Change made: Section 3.3 and AI disclosure statement updated.
