AI pitfalls and real cases

The ways AI output goes wrong in academic writing, and published cases where it did.

Gallery of common AI errors

Illustrative examples written for this workshop, based on patterns that appear in real AI output.

Fabricated reference

Your request: “Add two recent references supporting this claim.”

AI output: “(Chen & Hassan, 2023, Journal of Educational AI Research, 12(3), 145–162)”, a confident, correctly formatted reference to a paper and journal that do not exist.

Prevent it: Never ask AI to supply citations. Check every DOI with the reference checker and open the source.

Meaning changed while paraphrasing

Original: “There was no significant difference between groups.”

AI output: “There was only a slight difference between the groups.”

Prevent it: Compare every polished sentence with the original, especially results. Ask the AI to list its changes.

Correlation turned into causation

Original: “Social influence was associated with intention.”

AI output: “Social influence drives students' intention.”

Prevent it: Use the hedging prompt. Match verbs to your design: cross-sectional data show association.

Terminology drift

Original: “behavioural intention” used throughout.

AI output: “usage”, “adoption” and “acceptance” used interchangeably in different paragraphs.

Prevent it: Tell the AI to keep construct names exactly as written, and search the final text for each key term.

Invented or altered numbers

Original: “47.6% of respondents”.

AI output: “the majority of respondents (over 50%)”.

Prevent it: Check every number in the polished text against your tables.

Overclaiming

Original: “The model fits the data well.”

AI output: “The model provides robust and conclusive evidence.”

Prevent it: Delete inflated adjectives and claims your design cannot support.

AI-sounding wording

Original: “This study examines …”

AI output: “This study delves into the intricate interplay of …”

Prevent it: Use the language checker and the words-to-avoid list.

Prompt leftovers

Pasted output still containing:

“Certainly! Here is a revised version of your paragraph:” or “Regenerate response”.

Prevent it: Search the final manuscript for leftovers before submitting. These have led to retractions.

Tortured phrases from paraphrasing tools

Original term: “artificial intelligence”.

Paraphrasing tool output: “counterfeit consciousness”; “big data” becomes “colossal information”.

Prevent it: Do not use paraphrasing tools to disguise text. Established terms must stay unchanged.

Published cases and retracted articles

Real cases and evidence, with full references and DOI links so you can read the articles and retraction notices yourself.

2023

Undisclosed ChatGPT use found through a leftover phrase

A paper in the IOP Publishing journal Physica Scripta contained the phrase “Regenerate response”, a button label from ChatGPT, pasted into the text. It was spotted by integrity researcher Guillaume Cabanac. The authors confirmed they had used ChatGPT, and the paper was retracted for failing to declare it. IOP Publishing called this “a breach of our ethical policies”.

Lesson: Check for leftovers, and disclose AI use even when it seems minor.

The article

  • Tarla, S., Ali, K. K., & Yusuf, A. (2023). Exploring new optical solutions for nonlinear Hamiltonian amplitude equation via two integration schemes. Physica Scripta, 98(9), 095218. https://doi.org/10.1088/1402-4896/aceb40 (Retracted)
  • Retraction: Exploring new optical solutions for nonlinear Hamiltonian amplitude equation via two integration schemes. (2023). Physica Scripta, 98(10), 109701. https://doi.org/10.1088/1402-4896/acf6b8 (Retraction notice)

Reported by: Nature news: Scientific sleuths spot dishonest ChatGPT use in papers · Futurism · PubPeer record

2024

AI-generated figures retracted three days after publication

An article in Frontiers in Cell and Developmental Biology included figures made with Midjourney, including an anatomically impossible rat and diagrams with nonsense labels. The paper was retracted on 16 February 2024. Frontiers said the figures broke its rules on AI-generated images and that its own checks had failed to catch them.

Lesson: AI images must be checked for accuracy and allowed by the journal; peer review will not always catch problems for you.

The article

  • Guo, X., Dong, L., & Hao, D. (2024). Cellular functions of spermatogonial stem cells in relation to JAK/STAT signaling pathway. Frontiers in Cell and Developmental Biology, 11, 1339390. https://doi.org/10.3389/fcell.2023.1339390 (Retracted)
  • Frontiers Editorial Office. (2024). Retraction: Cellular functions of spermatogonial stem cells in relation to JAK/STAT signaling pathway. Frontiers in Cell and Developmental Biology, 12, 1386861. https://doi.org/10.3389/fcell.2024.1386861 (Retraction notice)

Reported by: Gizmodo · Phys.org

2023

Measured: how often ChatGPT fabricates citations

Walters and Wilder asked GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 to write 84 short literature reviews containing 636 citations. 55% of GPT-3.5 citations and 18% of GPT-4 citations were fabricated, and many real citations contained substantive errors (43% and 24%).

Lesson: Even newer models fabricate references. Every reference must be verified.

The article

  • Walters, W. H., & Wilder, E. I. (2023). Fabrication and errors in the bibliographic citations generated by ChatGPT. Scientific Reports, 13, 14045. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-41032-5 (Open access)

2021–2022

Tortured phrases lead to mass retractions

Researchers Guillaume Cabanac, Cyril Labbé and Alexander Magazinov identified “tortured phrases” such as “counterfeit consciousness” for artificial intelligence, produced by paraphrasing software used to disguise copied text. Their Problematic Paper Screener flags such papers. Elsevier retracted or removed 165 articles from Microprocessors and Microsystems, and IOP Publishing announced the retraction of 350 conference papers.

Lesson: Paraphrasing tools used to evade plagiarism checks leave detectable traces.

Reported by: Revista Pesquisa FAPESP

Detecting AI-generated text in published papers

Chatbot phrases such as “As an AI language model” and “I’m very sorry, but I don’t have access to real-time information” have survived peer review and copy-editing in published articles. A simple search finds them.

Try it yourself: search Google Scholar for “As an AI language model”. Before you submit, search your own manuscript for “As an AI”, “Certainly”, “Regenerate response” and “I hope this helps”, or paste it into the language checker.
