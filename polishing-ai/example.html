Worked example: one abstract, five rounds

A draft abstract polished in five rounds. Each round uses one focused prompt, and the author decides what to keep. Deleted text is struck through in red; inserted text is green.

Round 1 of 5

Structure check

AI reviews the structure; the author does the rewriting.

Prompt used

Act as the handling editor of [JOURNAL]. Review my abstract against this structure: background, aim, method, key findings, contribution. Identify any missing or weak element. Do not rewrite it.

What came back

  • No research gap: the abstract goes straight from the topic to the aim.
  • Method lacks the sampling approach and does not say UTAUT was extended.
  • No measure of explanatory power.
  • The contribution (“help university to make policy”) is generic.

Author's decision

The author added the gap, the sampling method, the model extension, R² and a more specific contribution, in their own words. The language is still rough; that comes next.

Round 2 of 5

Language polish

AI improves grammar and flow under strict rules.

Prompt used

Act as an academic editor for a Q1 journal in educational technology. Improve the clarity, grammar and flow of the abstract below. Do not change the meaning or add new claims. Keep technical terms exactly as written. Use British English. Return the revised text and a table of changes.

What came back

  • Fixed grammar, articles, plurals and verb agreement throughout.
  • Spelled out UTAUT and PLS-SEM at first use.
  • Introduced “pivotal issue” and “delves into”.
  • Kept “This study proves”, an overclaim.

Author's decision

Accepted the grammar fixes. Noted the two AI-sounding phrases and the overclaim for later rounds instead of accepting the text as finished.

Round 3 of 5

Hedging and claims

AI flags claims that go beyond the evidence.

Prompt used

Identify every sentence that makes a claim stronger than the evidence supports. For each, suggest a more appropriately hedged version. Do not change sentences that report results factually.

What came back

  • “significantly affect” implies causation from cross-sectional survey data.
  • “has a negative effect” has the same problem.
  • “This study proves” is too strong for a single survey.

Author's decision

Accepted all three, because a cross-sectional design supports association, not causation.

Round 4 of 5

Style check

Remove wording that sounds generic or machine-generated.

Prompt used

Review the text for overused words, filler and inflated adjectives (such as delve, pivotal, intricate). List each with a plainer alternative. Keep my technical terms.

What came back

  • “pivotal issue” → a more specific description of the problem.
  • “delves into” → “examines”.

Author's decision

Rewrote the opening sentence to say what the issue actually is (assessment), rather than calling it “pivotal”.

Round 5 of 5

Final human check

The author reads against the data and the paper.

Prompt used

No prompt. The author compares the abstract with the results tables and the rest of the manuscript.

What came back

  • Past tense for a completed analysis: “explains” → “explained”, with R² reported.
  • Terminology drift: the AI had written “AI usage”, but the study measured intention, not use.
  • The last sentence was still generic, so the author tied it to the two strongest findings.
  • “in the Malaysian context” shortened to “in Malaysia”.

Author's decision

Only the author could catch the terminology drift, because it needs knowledge of what was actually measured.

Final abstract

Generative AI chatbots are increasingly used by university students, which raises new questions for assessment in higher education. However, little is known about what drives undergraduate students to use these tools for their assignments in Malaysia. This study examines the factors influencing undergraduate students' intention to use generative AI for assignments by extending the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) with perceived risk. Data were collected from 412 students at three public universities in Malaysia through an online questionnaire using stratified sampling and analysed using partial least squares structural equation modelling (PLS-SEM). Performance expectancy, effort expectancy and social influence were significant predictors of behavioural intention, whereas facilitating conditions were not. Perceived risk was negatively associated with behavioural intention. The model explained 58% of the variance in behavioural intention (R² = .58). These findings suggest that the extended UTAUT offers a useful framework for explaining students' intention to use generative AI. Universities can use these findings to design assessment policies that weigh students' expectations of usefulness against their perceived risks.

What this example shows

One prompt per job works better than “improve my abstract”. AI fixed the language quickly but also added AI-sounding phrases, kept an overclaim, and drifted from “intention” to “usage”. The structural and terminology decisions stayed with the author.
