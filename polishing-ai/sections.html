Weak vs stronger: section by section

The same study written two ways. Use these to brief an AI tool on what “better” means for each section, or to check your own draft.

Fictional example written for this workshop. Any resemblance to a real study is coincidental.

TitleAbstract: findingsIntroduction: research gapLiterature reviewMethodsResultsDiscussionLimitationsConclusion

Title

Weak

A study on the factors of AI usage among students in Malaysia

Stronger

Perceived risk and students' intention to use generative AI for assignments: an extended UTAUT model

Why it works: The stronger title names the key constructs and the theory, so it is searchable, and it says what the study is about rather than that it is “a study”.

Abstract: findings

Weak

The results show many important findings that are significant for education.

Stronger

Performance expectancy and social influence predicted intention to use generative AI, while facilitating conditions did not (R² = .58).

Why it works: Reviewers want the actual findings and their size, not a statement that findings exist.

Introduction: research gap

Weak

Many studies have been done on technology acceptance. However, there is still a gap in this area that needs to be studied.

Stronger

Most UTAUT studies of generative AI have sampled students in North America and Europe and have not modelled perceived risk, even though concerns about academic integrity are central to students' decisions (Author, 2024; Author, 2025).

Why it works: A gap must say exactly what is missing and why it matters, with citations that support the claim.

Literature review

Weak

Lee (2023) found that usefulness affects intention. Tan (2024) also found that usefulness affects intention. Kumar (2024) studied ease of use and found it was significant.

Stronger

Perceived usefulness consistently predicts intention to use generative AI (Kumar, 2024; Lee, 2023; Tan, 2024), whereas findings on ease of use are mixed, possibly because chat interfaces require little technical skill.

Why it works: Synthesis groups studies by finding and explains disagreement; a list of summaries leaves that work to the reader.

Methods

Weak

Questionnaires were distributed to students and the data were analysed using SmartPLS.

Stronger

An online questionnaire was distributed to undergraduate students at three public universities using stratified sampling by faculty (n = 412; response rate 61%). The model was estimated with PLS-SEM in SmartPLS 4 using 5,000 bootstrap subsamples.

Why it works: Methods must be detailed enough for replication: sampling, sample size, response rate, software version and key settings.

Results

Weak

Performance expectancy has a very strong and important effect on intention, which proves that students value usefulness.

Stronger

Performance expectancy was positively associated with behavioural intention (β = .41, p < .001).

Why it works: Results report what was found, with statistics. Interpretation such as “students value usefulness” belongs in the discussion.

Discussion

Weak

This study found that performance expectancy is significant. Effort expectancy is also significant. Social influence is significant too.

Stronger

Consistent with Lee (2023), usefulness was the strongest predictor. Unlike studies in Western settings, however, social influence was also significant, which may reflect the collective norms of Malaysian classrooms. This suggests that institutional guidance from lecturers may shape adoption more than tool design.

Why it works: A discussion compares findings with the literature, explains differences and answers “so what?”, rather than repeating the results.

Limitations

Weak

This study has some limitations. Future studies should be done.

Stronger

Because the data are cross-sectional and self-reported, causal relationships cannot be established. Longitudinal studies that link intention to observed use in assessed work would address this.

Why it works: A useful limitation names the weakness, explains its consequence and suggests a specific way forward.

Conclusion

Weak

In conclusion, AI is very important and this study has given a lot of contributions to the body of knowledge.

Stronger

Students' intention to use generative AI for assignments depends on expected usefulness, peer norms and perceived risk. Assessment policies that address these three factors are more likely to shape responsible use than bans alone.

Why it works: The conclusion restates the main answer and its implication in plain terms, without new information or vague claims of contribution.
