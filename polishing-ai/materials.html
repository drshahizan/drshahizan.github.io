Activity materials

Materials for the six hands-on activities. Facilitator notes and answer keys are hidden until you open them.

1. Before and after

Use your own abstract. If you do not have one, use the starting draft from the worked example. Run one round at a time using the prompt builder, then compare versions.

2. Prompt challenge

Write a prompt that improves this paragraph without changing its meaning. Post your prompt and result in the Webex chat.

In this research, the data is collected by using the interview with 15 lecturers. The interview was conduct in face to face and online and each interview take around 45 minute. After that the data was transcribe and analyse using thematic analysis by following Braun and Clarke (2006) six steps. From the analysis, there are four theme was found which is workload, trust, skill and policy.
What a good result keeps

15 lecturers; face-to-face and online interviews; about 45 minutes each; transcribed; Braun and Clarke (2006) six phases; four themes named exactly as workload, trust, skill and policy. A good prompt tells the AI not to add details such as sampling or software that are not in the original.

  2. Problem Fabricated. The DOI, volume and pages belong to LeCun et al. (2015). A real DOI attached to an invented paper is a common AI error.
  3. Genuine
  4. Problem Real paper, wrong journal. It was published in Science, 379(6630), 313. The DOI prefix 10.1126 (Science) is the clue. A title-only check would miss this.
  5. Genuine
  6. Problem Fabricated. The journal does not exist and there is no DOI. Plausible author names and a topical title make it convincing.

  8. Problem Fabricated. Real authors and a real journal, but the paper does not exist. The anachronism is a clue: generative AI chatbots became widespread from late 2022.

4. Desk review simulation

You are the editor. Read the journal scope, then decide for each abstract: send to review, or desk reject. Give one reason.

Journal scope

Journal of Digital Learning in Higher Education (fictional) publishes empirical studies on how digital technologies affect teaching, learning and assessment in universities. Studies must make a clear contribution to theory or practice beyond a single institution. Out of scope: school (K-12) settings, technical system descriptions without evaluation, and opinion pieces.

Fictional example written for this workshop. Any resemblance to a real study is coincidental.

Abstract A

Generative AI tools are increasingly used to give students feedback on academic writing, yet little is known about how this feedback compares with lecturer feedback in shaping revision. This mixed-methods study examined 186 undergraduate essays from writing courses at four universities in Malaysia and Vietnam. Each essay received either AI-generated or lecturer feedback, and revisions were scored by two blinded raters (ICC = .87). Students receiving lecturer feedback made more substantive revisions to argument structure (d = 0.48), whereas AI feedback led to more surface-level language changes. Interviews with 24 students suggested that they trusted AI feedback on language but not on content. The findings refine feedback literacy theory by distinguishing trust in feedback by domain, and suggest that AI feedback should complement rather than replace lecturer feedback on argumentation.

Facilitator note

Send to review. In scope (universities, assessment), multi-institution, clear design, effect sizes reported, and a stated theoretical contribution.

Abstract B

Tablets are now common in primary schools. This study surveyed 220 pupils aged 9–11 in two primary schools to examine their enjoyment of mathematics apps. Pupils who used the apps more than three times a week reported higher enjoyment of mathematics (p < .05). Teachers also reported that pupils were more engaged. The findings suggest that schools should invest in mathematics apps to improve learning.

Facilitator note

Desk reject: out of scope (primary school setting). It also measures enjoyment but recommends investment to “improve learning”, which the data do not support.

Abstract C

AI is changing education in many ways. In this study, we used ChatGPT in one course at our university and asked students about their experience. The results show that students like ChatGPT very much and it improves their learning. This study proves that ChatGPT is a very useful tool and all universities should use it. This research gives a significant contribution to the body of knowledge.

Facilitator note

Desk reject or return. Single course and institution, no theory, no method details, no numbers, and claims (“proves”, “all universities should”) far beyond a satisfaction survey.
