Understand the data before you use it

In this lab you will explore one official dataset with Google Sheets. The goal is not complex analysis. It is to understand what the data means, where it comes from, what it can and cannot tell you, and who uses it.

At a glance

WhenIn class, Tuesday 17 November 2026 (about 2 hours)
WorkIn pairs; one shared Google Sheet per pair
ToolGoogle Sheets with your @graduate.utm.my account
DatasetPopulation Table: Malaysia ↗ · Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM) · CC BY 4.0
AssessmentPractice only. It is not graded, but you may keep it as evidence in your E-Portfolio.

Learning outcomes

  • Read a data dictionary and metadata: producer, unit, granularity, coverage and licence.
  • Recognise common data traps: totals mixed with details, units, rounding, changing categories and scope.
  • Turn data into information with simple charts, and explain who would use it.
  • Map the data ecosystem behind an official dataset: sources, producer, publisher, access channels, users and roles.

Before you start (10 min)

Open the dataset page

Go to data.gov.my › Population Table: Malaysia ↗ and read the description, the data dictionary and the methodology notes.

Load the data into Google Sheets

Create a new Google Sheet. In cell A1, type the formula below and press Enter. The full table (about 17,800 rows) will load. If it does not, download the CSV from the dataset page and use File › Import.

=IMPORTDATA("https://storage.dosm.gov.my/population/population_malaysia.csv")

Turn on a filter

Select row 1, then Data › Create a filter. You will use the filters on date, sex, age and ethnicity throughout the lab.

Part A · 15 min

Read the data card first

Before looking at any numbers, answer these from the dataset page:

  1. Who produced the data, and who publishes it?
  2. What is the unit of the population column?
  3. What does one row represent (granularity)? How often is it measured?
  4. Which years are covered?
  5. Which years are census figures and which are estimates?
  6. What is the licence, and what does it allow you to do?
  7. When is the next update?
  8. Which caveats (warnings) does the page give?
i

Columns: date (1 January of each year), sex (both, male, female), age (overall or five-year groups), ethnicity (overall or ethnic groups) and population (in thousands).

Part B · 40 min

Data traps

Answer each question in your Google Sheet first. Then open Show answer and discuss the lesson with your partner.

1What was the population of Malaysia in 2026?

Show answer

Filter date = 2026-01-01, sex = both, age = overall and ethnicity = overall. The answer is 34,389.4 (thousand).

Lesson: If you simply add up every 2026 row, you get about 275,115: eight times too large. The table repeats the same people in many rows (totals, by sex, by age, by ethnicity and their combinations). Always find the total row instead of summing everything.

2Write that answer as a number of people.

Show answer

About 34.4 million people (34,389.4 × 1,000 = 34,389,400).

Lesson: The population column is in thousands. Reading 34,389.4 as “34 thousand people” is a unit error. Always check the unit in the data dictionary.

3Add the 18 age groups for 2026 (sex = both, ethnicity = overall). Is the sum equal to the total?

Show answer

The age groups add up to 34,389.2, not 34,389.4. The difference is 0.2 thousand.

Lesson: Each value is rounded to one decimal place, so the parts may not add up exactly to the total. This is a documented caveat, not an error in your spreadsheet.

4How many people aged 85 and over were there in 1985?

Show answer

There is no answer. In 1985 the oldest group is 80+ (90.0 thousand). The 85+ group only starts in 1991.

Lesson: Category definitions change over time: 70+ in the 1970s, 80+ from 1980 to 1990 and 85+ from 1991. You cannot compare age groups that do not exist in both years.

5Compare the share of the Chinese population in 1975 and 2025.

Show answer

It cannot be done for 1975: the years 1970–1979 have no ethnic breakdown. You can compare 1985 (29.9%) with 2026 (19.9%).

Lesson: Missing breakdowns are not zeros. Check which years have which categories before you compare.

6In 2026, about 9.8% of the population is other_noncitizen. Can you find this group in 1985?

Show answer

No. In 1980–1990 there is only one combined group, other. Citizens and non-citizens are separated only from 1991.

Lesson: Categories can be split or renamed over time (for example, bumi becomes bumi_malay and bumi_other in 1991). A data engineer must map old and new categories carefully.

7Is the figure for 2023 as reliable as the figure for 2020?

Show answer

Not in the same way. 2020 is a census year (adjusted census figure). 2023 is an estimate calculated from the previous year plus births, minus deaths, plus net migration.

Lesson: Knowing how data was produced (its lineage or methodology) tells you how much to trust it and how it may be revised later.

8What was the population of Johor in 2026?

Show answer

This dataset cannot answer it. It is national-level only.

Lesson: Every dataset has a scope. To answer a state-level question you need a different dataset, such as the state population table in the data.gov.my catalogue.

Part C · 30 min

From data to information

Create three charts in Google Sheets (Insert › Chart). Filter the data first so that each chart uses only the rows it needs.

ChartFilterChart type
1 · Population trend, 1970–2026sex = both · age = overall · ethnicity = overallLine chart
2 · Population pyramid, 2026date = 2026 · sex = male and female · ethnicity = overall · age ≠ overallBar chart (males as negative values)
3 · Ethnic composition, 2026date = 2026 · sex = both · age = overall · ethnicity ≠ overallPie or bar chart

For each chart, write one finding and who would use it, for example the Ministry of Health, the Ministry of Education, town planners or businesses.

Need ideas? Things to look for
  • How many times larger is the population in 2026 than in 1970?
  • Compare the share of children aged 0–14 and adults aged 65+ in 1970 and in 2026. What does this mean for schools and health care?
  • Which age groups are the largest in 2026?
  • How many males are there for every 100 females in 2026?
Part D · 25 min

Map the data ecosystem

Draw the journey of this dataset from source to user, on paper or in a shared slide.

Sources→Producer→Publisher→Access channels→Users
  • Sources: where could the data come from? Think about the census, birth and death registration and migration records.
  • Producer and publisher: who processes the data, and where is it shared?
  • Access channels: the same data is offered as CSV, Parquet and an API. Why offer three ways?
  • Users: who uses population data, and for what decisions?
  • People and roles: label where a data producer, data engineer, data steward, data analyst and data consumer would work.
  • Data maturity: find two signs that this dataset is well managed (for example, a data dictionary, an open licence, an update schedule or methodology notes).

Link your map to The Ecosystem, People and Roles, Data Journey and Data Maturity.
