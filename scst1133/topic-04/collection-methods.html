Two Families of Methods

Ask people directly, or study what already exists. Good analysts use both.

Interactive and unobtrusive methods

Before designing any data solution, analysts gather information about problems, opportunities, requirements and priorities. This is called fact-finding or requirements discovery.

AspectInteractive methodsUnobtrusive methods
DefinitionInvolve direct interaction with stakeholdersGather information passively or from existing sources, without direct interaction
PurposeIn-depth, qualitative insight into users’ needsLarge-scale or structured evidence, often factual or historical
ExamplesInterviews, focus groups, Joint Application Design (JAD), questionnaires, brainstormingSampling, document analysis, observation, system-log analysis, data mining
When to useDetailed insight is needed; user input is crucial; validating findingsHistorical or factual data is needed; access to people is difficult; minimising respondent bias matters
StrengthsRich detail; allows clarifying and probing questionsLess disruptive; more objective; cost-effective at scale
LimitationsTime-consuming; may be biased by the interviewer; needs arrangingMay lack depth; data may be outdated or incomplete; hard to interpret without context

Use more than one method

Unobtrusive methods are less disruptive but are rarely enough on their own. Analysts combine them with interactive methods—for example, observing a process and then interviewing the people involved to understand why they work that way.

i

Language note: The correct term is unobtrusive (not intrusive), not “obstructive”.

Source: Lecture slides, based on Kendall, K. E. & Kendall, J. E. (2020). Systems Analysis and Design (10th ed.). Pearson.
