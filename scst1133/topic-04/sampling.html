Sampling

You rarely need to examine everything. A well-chosen sample saves time, money and bias.

05Sampling
Unobtrusive method

What to examine, and whom to consider

Sampling selects a representative part of a population of people, documents or events. It contains costs, speeds up data collection and reduces bias.

Four sampling decisions

Determine the data

Decide what you need to collect or describe.

Determine the population

Identify all the people, documents or events of interest.

Choose the sampling type

Convenience, purposive, simple random or complex random.

Decide the sample size

Calculate how many items are needed.

Not based on probabilityBased on probability
Selected directly, without restrictionsConvenience — easy to arrange but the most unreliableSimple random — every item has an equal chance; needs a numbered list of the population
Selected according to criteriaPurposive — based on judgement about who is knowledgeable; moderately reliableComplex random — systematic, stratified or cluster sampling; recommended when possible

Calculating sample size

  1. Determine the expected proportion with the attribute (for example, errors), p.
  2. Determine the acceptable interval estimate, i.
  3. Choose a confidence level and look up its coefficient, z (for 95%, z = 1.96).
  4. Calculate the standard error of the proportion: σp = i ÷ z.
  5. Calculate the sample size: n = p(1 − p) ÷ σp² + 1, rounding up.

Worked example: a toy manufacturer expects 5% of toys to be broken during packaging (p = 0.05), accepts an interval estimate of 0.02 and wants 95% confidence. σp = 0.02 ÷ 1.96 ≈ 0.0102, so n = 0.05 × 0.95 ÷ 0.0102² + 1 ≈ 458 toys.
